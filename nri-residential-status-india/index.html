NRI Residential Status in India: Rules, Stay Limits and Tax Implications

A comprehensive practitioner's guide to physical stay calculations under Section 6, the 120-day rule, RNOR transitional status, deemed residency, and documentation essentials for overseas Indians.

Authored by RRC Law Associates
Published: November 2026
13 min read
Verified for 2026 Compliance
Statutory determination of physical presence, immigration day counts, and cross-border income scope under Indian direct tax law.

For Non-Resident Indians (NRIs), understanding residential status is an important part of managing income-tax obligations in India. Living and working abroad does not automatically determine your tax status.

Indian direct tax jurisprudence evaluates taxability based on physical presence in India during each financial year (1 April to 31 March), rather than foreign citizenship, visa classification, or green card status. An inadvertent overstay or misunderstanding of arrival and departure rules can transform a non-resident into an Indian tax resident, exposing global income to scrutiny.

1. What Is Residential Status Under Indian Income-Tax Law?

Under the Income-tax Act, 1961, tax liability in India is established by residential status, not citizenship or domicile. An Indian citizen who resides abroad may be treated as a resident for tax purposes if they spend sufficient days in India, while a foreign citizen may be treated as a non-resident.

Residential status governs the scope of total income under Section 5 of the Act:

  • Territorial Scope: Non-Residents are taxed strictly on income that is received, accrues, or is deemed to accrue or arise in India. Overseas salary, foreign business profits, and foreign rental income remain completely outside the Indian tax net.
  • Worldwide Scope: Residents and Ordinarily Residents (ROR) are subject to tax on their global worldwide income, regardless of where it is earned or received.
  • Annual Determination: Residential status is never permanent. It is assessed anew for each financial year based on your travel calendar between 1 April and 31 March.

2. How Is NRI Residential Status Determined?

Section 6(1) of the Income-tax Act provides the fundamental statutory tests for individuals. An individual is classified as a Resident in India during a financial year if they satisfy either of the following two basic conditions:

Basic Condition A

Physical stay in India for an aggregate period of 182 days or more during the relevant financial year.

Basic Condition B

Physical stay in India for 60 days or more during the financial year AND 365 days or more during the preceding 4 financial years.

If an individual satisfies neither Condition A nor Condition B, they qualify legally as a Non-Resident (NR) for that financial year.

Critical Exceptions to Condition B (Relaxation Rules)

To protect Indians working overseas, the law modifies the 60-day threshold under Condition B in specific circumstances:

  • Indian Citizens Leaving for Employment Abroad: An Indian citizen who leaves India during the financial year for employment abroad or as a member of the crew of an Indian ship is tested solely against the 182-day rule (the 60-day threshold is substituted by 182 days).
  • Indian Citizens or PIOs Visiting India (General Rule): An Indian citizen or Person of Indian Origin (PIO) residing abroad who visits India is entitled to stay up to 181 days without triggering residency under Condition B (the 60 days are substituted by 182 days).
  • The 120-Day Rule for High-Income NRIs: If an Indian citizen or PIO visiting India has total Indian taxable income (excluding foreign sources) exceeding INR 15 Lakhs, the 60-day threshold is substituted by 120 days. If their stay reaches 120 days or more (and 365 days in the prior 4 years), they lose pure non-resident status and become Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR).

Deemed Residency Under Section 6(1A)

Introduced to address stateless individuals and residents of zero-tax jurisdictions (such as the UAE, Qatar, or Oman), Section 6(1A) states that an individual being an Indian citizen whose total Indian taxable income exceeds INR 15 Lakhs shall be deemed to be a resident (specifically RNOR) in India if they are not liable to tax in any other country by reason of their domicile or residence.

3. Difference Between NR, RNOR and ROR

Indian tax law recognizes three distinct residential classifications for individuals. Understanding the boundaries between them is essential to prevent unintended exposure of foreign assets:

Tax Category Indian Sourced Income Foreign Sourced Income Foreign Asset Disclosure (Schedule FA)
Non-Resident (NR) Taxable in India 100% Exempt Not Required
Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR) Taxable in India Exempt (unless derived from business controlled in India) Not Required
Resident and Ordinarily Resident (ROR) Taxable in India Taxable Globally Mandatory Reporting

How Does an Individual Become RNOR?

A resident individual is classified as an RNOR if they satisfy either of the following tests under Section 6(6):

  • They have been a Non-Resident in 9 out of the 10 preceding financial years; OR
  • They have been in India for a total period of 729 days or less during the 7 preceding financial years.

RNOR status acts as a crucial transitional shield for returning NRIs, giving them up to 2–3 tax years of exemption on their overseas investments and foreign pensions.

4. Why Is the Number of Days Spent in India Important?

The calculation of days is rigorous and strictly mathematical. Unlike subjective immigration rules, the Income Tax Department computes physical presence hour-by-hour based on passport movement records:

Counting Arrival and Departure Days

Under established judicial precedent affirmed by the High Courts and Supreme Court (including CIT v. Manoj Kumar Reddy), both the day of arrival and the day of departure are counted as full days spent in India, regardless of whether you land at 11:50 PM or depart at 1:10 AM.

For example, an NRI arriving in Chennai on 1 November and departing on 10 November has spent 10 days in India for tax purposes, not 8 or 9 days. In close cases near the 119-day or 181-day thresholds, miscounting a single day can trigger residency, invalidating non-resident tax exemptions.

5. How Does Residential Status Affect Indian Income Tax?

Your residential status directly dictates your tax bracket, withholding rates, and statutory obligations:

  • Bank Interest & Section 10(4)(ii): Interest on Non-Resident External (NRE) and Foreign Currency Non-Resident (FCNR) accounts is 100% tax-free only while you maintain non-resident status under the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA) and the Income-tax Act. If your status shifts to resident, this exemption lapses.
  • Withholding Tax (TDS) Under Section 195: When paying income to an NRI (such as rental income, capital gains on property sale, or NRO interest), the payer must deduct TDS at peak rates (typically 20% to 30% plus applicable surcharge and cess). Residents, by contrast, are subject to lower domestic TDS (e.g., 10% under Section 194A or Section 194-I).
  • Special Concessional Tax Regime (Chapter XII-A): Non-residents investing foreign currency in specified Indian assets (shares, debentures, government bonds) enjoy simplified, flat tax rates under Sections 115C through 115-I.
  • Filing Form ITR-2 or ITR-3: NRIs are legally barred from filing simplified Form ITR-1 (Sahaj). They must file ITR-2 or ITR-3, detailing residential status calculations and movement history.

6. What Documents Should NRIs Maintain?

In the event of scrutiny under Section 143(2) or reopening under Section 148, the burden of proving non-resident status rests entirely on the taxpayer. Overseas Indians should maintain a dedicated compliance file containing:

  • Passport Pages & Immigration Stamps: Clear color scans of all passport pages (current and previous expired passports covering the last 7 years) displaying entry and exit stamps from Indian immigration and overseas border control.
  • Flight Itineraries & Boarding Passes: Electronic tickets, e-visa confirmations, and boarding cards that substantiate precise flight timing.
  • Overseas Residence & Employment Contracts: Valid work visas (H-1B, L-1, Tier 2, Blue Card, Iqama, UAE Golden Visa), employment contracts, overseas utility bills, or lease agreements confirming ordinary residence outside India.
  • Tax Residency Certificate (TRC): An official certificate issued by the revenue agency of your country of residence (such as IRS Form 6166 in the US, HMRC certificate in the UK, or FTA certificate in the UAE).
  • Indian & Foreign Bank Statements: Annual statements for all NRE, NRO, and FCNR accounts demonstrating remittance sources and local debits.

7. Important Change From 1 April 2026

The legislative landscape governing Indian direct taxation is evolving. It is crucial to understand the prospective application of new statutory frameworks:

Prospective Application of the Income-tax Act, 2025

The Income-tax Act, 2025 applies exclusively to tax years beginning on or after 1 April 2026 (Assessment Year 2027-28 onwards). Earlier tax years (including FY 2024-25 and FY 2025-26) continue to be governed strictly by the Income-tax Act, 1961, subject to transitional provisions under Section 395(1).

Under the 2025 Act, residency determination rules under Section 6 are restructured into clearer statutory language, digital verification of travel days through automated immigration data integration is codified, and transitional continuity is protected. Non-residents should ensure that filings for earlier years adhere strictly to the 1961 Act framework.

8. Common Mistakes NRIs Should Avoid

In our practice at RRC Law Associates, we frequently counsel overseas clients facing avoidable tax assessments resulting from these common errors:

  1. Confusing FEMA NRI Status with Income Tax Residency: FEMA residency is based on your intent to stay abroad for an uncertain period. Income tax residency is based purely on mechanical day-counting. You can be an NRI under FEMA but a resident for Income Tax in a year you visit India for extended family reasons.
  2. Ignoring the 120-Day Rule When Indian Income Exceeds INR 15 Lakhs: An NRI visiting India who stays 125 days and earns INR 18 Lakhs in Indian rental income inadvertently becomes an RNOR, losing non-resident benefits.
  3. Omitting Arrival and Departure Days: Calculating stay by subtracting dates without counting both endpoint days results in an undercount of 2 days per trip.
  4. Maintaining Resident Savings Accounts: Failing to convert ordinary domestic savings accounts to NRO accounts upon moving abroad violates FEMA and triggers Automated Information Return (AIR) scrutiny from the tax department.
  5. Failing to Obtain a Tax Residency Certificate (TRC): Relying on foreign utility bills or driver licenses instead of a formal TRC when seeking treaty relief under Section 90.
