HSN Classification Disputes and Customs Tariff Interpretation

General Rules for Interpretation (GRI 1 to 6), Chapter Notes, Trade Parlance Tests, and Customs Advance Rulings (CAAR)

13 min read
Reviewed by RRC Customs Litigation Practice
Updated November 2026

Key Takeaways & At a Glance

  • Classification of imported and exported goods is governed strictly by the Harmonized System of Nomenclature (HSN) under the Customs Tariff Act, 1975.
  • The General Rules for the Interpretation (GRI) must be applied sequentially from Rule 1 through Rule 6; you cannot jump to Rule 3 or Rule 4 if classification is resolved under Rule 1.
  • Under GRI 1, the statutory terms of the Headings, Section Notes, and Chapter Notes have overriding legal force over marketing terms, sales brochures, or generic descriptions.
  • Where an item cannot be classified strictly by its technical definition, Indian courts apply the 'Common Parlance Test' (Trade Meaning Test): how do commercial buyers, sellers, and trade professionals understand the product in the ordinary course of business?
  • Businesses can eliminate classification risks prior to shipping by securing a binding Advance Ruling from the Customs Authority for Advance Rulings (CAAR) under Section 28H.

Understanding the Legal Issue

In the day-to-day administration of Indian customs, no single area generates more persistent dispute, high-stakes duty demands, and appellate litigation than tariff classification under the Harmonized Commodity Description and Coding System (HSN). A difference of a single digit in an 8-digit HSN code can determine whether an imported consignment is subject to zero customs duty under an exemption notification, or exposed to standard basic customs duty, integrated GST (IGST), and anti-dumping duty exceeding 40%.

Tariff classification is not an arbitrary administrative exercise or an intuitive guessing game. It is a rigorous legal discipline governed by the First Schedule to the Customs Tariff Act, 1975, statutory Section and Chapter Notes, the six General Rules for the Interpretation (GRI) of the Harmonized System, and World Customs Organization (WCO) Explanatory Notes. When the Customs Appraising Group or DRI proposes to reject an importer's declared HSN code and reclassify goods under a higher-duty heading, mounting a technically sound, legally verified defense is essential to safeguarding business margins.

Key Provisions of the Customs Act, 1962

Customs proceedings in India are governed strictly by codified parliamentary statutes and binding notifications issued by the Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs (CBIC). The foundational statutory provisions applicable to this matter include:

Customs Tariff Act, 1975 First & Second Schedules

Statutory basis of the 8-digit Indian tariff classification system aligned with the international WCO Harmonized System.

GRI 1 to 6 General Rules for Interpretation

Mandatory sequential rules governing the legal classification of goods, establishing the supremacy of Section and Chapter Notes.

Section 28H Customs Act, 1962

Authorizes prospective importers and investors to apply for binding Customs Advance Rulings (CAAR) on classification and applicable duty rates.

In-Depth Legal Analysis & Practical Application

1. The Sequential Architecture of the General Rules for Interpretation (GRI)

The six GRI rules must be applied in strict numerical hierarchy:

  • GRI 1 (Primary Rule): Classification is determined legally by the terms of the headings and any relative Section or Chapter Notes. If a heading and its notes resolve the classification, no further rule is applied.
  • GRI 2(a) (Incomplete/Unfinished Articles): An incomplete article having the 'essential character' of the complete article is classified under the heading for the finished good.
  • GRI 2(b) (Mixtures/Combinations): Covers goods consisting of more than one material or substance.
  • GRI 3 (Composite Goods & Multiple Headings):
  • *Rule 3(a)*: The most specific description shall be preferred to headings providing a more general description.
  • *Rule 3(b)*: Composite goods and retail sets are classified according to the material or component that gives them their essential character.
  • *Rule 3(c)*: If 3(a) and 3(b) fail, classify under the heading that occurs last in numerical order.
  • GRI 4 (Akin Rule): Goods that cannot be classified under Rules 1 to 3 are classified under the heading appropriate to goods to which they are most akin.
  • GRI 5 (Cases & Containers): Specific packaging, camera cases, and musical instrument boxes shaped to contain the item are classified with the goods.
  • GRI 6 (Sub-heading Classification): Governs classification between 6-digit and 8-digit sub-headings based on sub-heading notes.

2. Legal Weight of Section Notes, Chapter Notes and WCO Explanatory Notes

The Supreme Court of India in landmark decisions (*Wood Craft Products, Phil Corporation, Holostick India*) established that:

  • Section Notes and Chapter Notes are statutory law enacted by Parliament; they possess absolute binding force and can alter the ordinary dictionary meaning of words.
  • WCO Harmonized System Explanatory Notes, while not formally part of Indian statutes, carry immense persuasive authority and are routinely adopted by Indian courts to resolve technical tariff ambiguities.

3. The Common Parlance / Trade Meaning Test

Where statutory notes do not explicitly define a term, Indian courts apply the 'Common Parlance Test': words must be construed in the sense in which people conversant with the trade or subject matter understand them. Technical laboratory definitions or academic dictionary meanings cannot displace commercial trade understanding.

Step-by-Step Legal Procedure

Navigating customs defense requires strict adherence to statutory sequences. The standard operational procedure followed by experienced customs litigation counsel includes:

Sequential Methodology for Resolving HSN Tariff Classification Disputes

1

Apply GIR Rules (GIR 1 to 6)

Classify according to Chapter and Section Notes (GIR 1); never jump to residual headings without exhausting specific descriptions (GIR 3a).

2

Technical & Lab Evidence

Compile chemical test reports, engineering drawings, manufacturer technical datasheets, and trade parlance affidavits.

3

WCO Explanatory Notes & Precedents

Incorporate World Customs Organization (WCO) Explanatory Notes and binding Supreme Court / CESTAT classification rulings.

4

Advance Ruling Application

Present legal submissions before the Principal Commissioner, or seek a proactive binding ruling from CAAR under Section 28H prior to import.

Essential Documents & Evidence Checklist

To substantiate claims, establish commercial bonafides, and refute departmental allegations, assemble the following documentary evidence:

  • Bill of Entry, Commercial Invoice, and Packing List
  • Manufacturer Technical Specification Sheets and Engineering Drawings
  • Chemical Analysis / Laboratory Test Reports (if applicable)
  • World Customs Organization (WCO) Explanatory Notes extracts
  • Certified Trade Affidavits confirming commercial understanding in the Indian market
  • Prior classification orders, CAAR rulings, or CESTAT precedents on identical products

Practical Case Scenario

Scenario: A medical technology importer in Coimbatore imported multifunction patient monitoring workstations. The importer declared HSN 9018 (Medical instruments, 0% Basic Customs Duty). Customs Appraising Group reclassified the consignment under HSN 8471 (Automatic Data Processing Machines / Computers, attracting 7.5% duty and additional cesses), demanding ₹1.2 Crores differential duty on the ground that the devices contained a standard CPU, RAM, and touch monitor.

Legal Resolution: RRC Law Associates defended the importer before the Commissioner (Appeals). We invoked Chapter Note 5(E) to Chapter 84, which explicitly states that machines incorporating or working in conjunction with an automatic data processing machine and performing a specific function other than data processing are to be classified in the headings proper to their specific functions. We demonstrated via medical council certifications that the essential function of the system was continuous physiological patient monitoring under Chapter 90, not general computing. The Commissioner (Appeals) accepted our statutory defense in full, quashing the reclassification and confirming the 0% duty medical heading.

Common Legal Pitfalls to Avoid

Critical Procedural Mistakes

  • Relying solely on overseas supplier HSN codes without verifying against the Indian 8-digit Customs Tariff and statutory Chapter Notes.
  • Jumping to GRI 3 (essential character) or GRI 4 without exhausting GRI 1 terms and statutory exclusions.
  • Failing to gather independent trade parlance evidence to rebut departmental laboratory test reports.
  • Accepting reclassification under protest without demanding a formal speaking order under Section 17(5) to preserve appellate rights.

Conclusion & Strategic Advice

Customs law disputes in India require swift, technically informed, and legally verified advocacy. Because customs authorities operate under stringent statutory limitation deadlines—from the 30-day Show Cause Notice window to the strict 60-day appellate period under Section 128—delay or informal verbal negotiations frequently compromise substantive legal rights.

Engaging specialized customs legal counsel at the earliest stage of search, summons, seizure, or notice issuance ensures that your business preserves documentary evidence, maintains procedural compliance, and enforces all statutory protections guaranteed under Indian law.
