Industrial Monopoly Rights

The Strategic Value of Indian Patent Grants

A patent is the most potent legal instrument in commercial enterprise. Granted under the Patents Act, 1970, it confers a 20-year legal monopoly preventing unauthorized manufacturing, using, offering for sale, selling, or importing the patented product or process within India.

Navigating patent prosecution demands rigorous techno-legal acumen. Applications must overcome stringent statutory novelty examinations, establish non-obvious inventive steps, and survive critical statutory hurdles such as Section 3(d) (incremental innovations lacking enhanced efficacy) and Section 3(k) (software per se).

Statutory Mandate

Statutory Rigor Under the Patents Act, 1970

The Indian patent system operates under the jurisdiction of the Controller General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks (CGPDTM). Applicants located in Tamil Nadu fall under the territorial jurisdiction of the Patent Office in Chennai.

India maintains strict public policy exclusions under Section 3 of the Act:

  • Section 3(d): Mere discovery of a new form of a known substance which does not result in the enhancement of the known efficacy.
  • Section 3(e): Mere admixtures resulting only in aggregation of properties.
  • Section 3(k): Mathematical methods, business methods, computer programmes per se, or algorithms.

20-Year Monopoly Rights

Under Section 48, a granted patent confers upon the patentee the exclusive right to prevent third parties, who do not have their consent, from the act of making, using, offering for sale, selling, or importing for those purposes the patented product or process.

Enforcement is instituted via civil infringement suits before the District Court or High Court under Section 104, seeking interim injunctions, damages, or an account of profits.

Important Notice: Patent prosecution involves strict statutory deadlines. Missing the 12-month complete filing or 6-month FER deadline results in statutory abandonment.

Territorial Scope Notice

An Indian patent grant provides legal monopoly exclusively within the geographical territory of India. There is no such thing as an automatic "world patent".

To protect inventions abroad, Indian inventors must file a PCT application or individual convention applications in the US, Europe, Japan, etc., within 12 months of their Indian priority date. Our patent team structures your global priority roadmap.

Clarifications & Guidance

Frequently Asked Questions

Statutory answers regarding patentability, specifications, and prosecution in India.

Under Section 2(1)(j) of the Patents Act, 1970, an invention must satisfy three core legal tests: (1) Novelty (it must not have been anticipated by prior publication or public use anywhere in the world); (2) Inventive Step / Non-Obviousness (it must involve technical advancement or economic significance that is not obvious to a person skilled in the art); and (3) Industrial Applicability (it must be capable of being made or used in an industry).

A Provisional Specification (Form 2) is filed to secure an immediate, legally recognized Priority Date while the invention is still undergoing R&D. It requires a clear technical description but does not require formal patent claims. A Complete Specification must be filed within 12 months of the provisional date; failing to file within this strict statutory deadline causes the application to be deemed abandoned.

Section 3(k) of the Patents Act strictly bars mathematical methods, business methods, computer programmes per se, and algorithms from patentability. However, computer-related inventions (CRIs) that produce a tangible technical effect or are inextricably tied to novel structural hardware components can be patented with appropriately crafted system and apparatus claims.

After a Request for Examination (Form 18) is filed, a Patent Examiner conducts a prior art search and issues a First Examination Report (FER) detailing statutory objections regarding novelty, inventive step, or formal drawing defects. The applicant has a mandatory, non-extendable statutory period of 6 months (extendable by 3 months upon Form 4 petition) to place the application in order for grant.

Under Section 53 of the Patents Act, 1970, every granted patent in India has a statutory term of 20 years from the date of filing of the application. For PCT National Phase applications, the 20-year term is calculated from the international filing date. Annual renewal fees must be paid to maintain the patent in force.

The PCT is an international treaty administered by WIPO allowing an applicant to seek patent protection simultaneously in over 155 countries by filing a single international application. Filing a PCT application gives inventors a 30 to 31-month window from the priority date to evaluate commercial viability before entering the national phase in individual target countries.
