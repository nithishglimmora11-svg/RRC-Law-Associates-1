NRI Income Tax Return Filing in India – Complete Guide

A definitive practitioner's guide to determining non-resident tax status, navigating territorial tax scope under Section 5(2), choosing between Old and New Tax Regimes, claiming high TDS refunds, and ensuring compliance under the Income-tax Act, 1961 and the incoming framework of the Income-tax Act, 2025.

Authored by RRC Law Associates
Published: October 2026
14 min read
Verified for 2026 Compliance
Statutory tax return filing and cross-border advisory for overseas Indians managing income and assets in India.

For millions of Non-Resident Indians (NRIs), Overseas Citizens of India (OCIs), and expatriates living across the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, the United Arab Emirates, Singapore, and Europe, managing financial interests in India is an ongoing reality. Whether deriving rental yields from ancestral properties, collecting interest on NRO deposits, realizing capital gains from real estate liquidations, or trading in domestic equity markets, understanding the statutory obligations of Indian direct taxation is essential.

Many non-residents erroneously believe that living abroad exempts them from filing an Income Tax Return (ITR) in India, or that having 30% Tax Deducted at Source (TDS) under Section 195 concludes their legal duties. In reality, failing to file can trigger automated scrutiny notices under Section 148, forfeit substantial tax refund entitlements, block funds from legal repatriation, and create cross-border double taxation friction.

1. Determining Residential Status Under Section 6

In Indian tax jurisprudence, taxability is dictated by residential status rather than citizenship. An Indian citizen holding an Indian passport may be treated as a non-resident, while a foreign citizen holding an OCI card may qualify as an Indian tax resident depending strictly on physical presence during the relevant financial year (April 1 to March 31).

Under Section 6 of the Income-tax Act, 1961 (and preserved in fundamental structure under the Income-tax Act, 2025), an individual is classified as a Resident in India during a financial year if they satisfy either of the following baseline conditions:

  • Condition A: Physical stay in India for an aggregate period of 182 days or more during the financial year; or
  • Condition B: Physical stay in India for 60 days or more during the financial year AND an aggregate stay of 365 days or more during the 4 preceding financial years.
Relaxation for Indian Citizens & NRIs Visiting India

For an Indian citizen or Person of Indian Origin (PIO) residing abroad who visits India during the year, the 60-day threshold in Condition B is extended to 182 days if their total Indian-source taxable income is up to INR 15 lakhs.

However, if Indian-sourced income (excluding foreign income) exceeds INR 15 lakhs, the threshold is reduced to 120 days. If physical presence falls between 120 and 181 days, the taxpayer is classified as Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR), conferring temporary exemption on foreign-sourced income while taxing all Indian earnings.

Furthermore, under Section 6(1A)—the "Deemed Resident" provision introduced to prevent stateless income planning—an Indian citizen whose total Indian taxable income exceeds INR 15 lakhs is deemed an RNOR if they are not liable to tax in any other country by reason of domicile, residence, or similar criteria (frequently relevant to professionals residing in zero-tax jurisdictions such as the UAE or Qatar).

2. Statutory Obligation: When Must an NRI File an ITR in India?

Every Non-Resident Indian is legally obligated to file an annual income tax return in India under Section 139(1) under specific statutory circumstances:

Mandatory Return Filing Triggers for NRIs
  • Gross Total Income Exceeds the Exemption Limit: If your total Indian income—before claiming Chapter VI-A deductions (such as Section 80C or 80D) or capital gains exemptions (under Sections 54, 54EC, or 54F)—exceeds the basic exemption threshold (INR 2,50,000 under the Old Regime / INR 3,00,000 under the New Regime).
  • Claiming TDS / TCS Refunds: If banks or property buyers deducted tax at source (often at steep flat rates of 20% to 30% plus surcharge and cess) and your actual computed liability is lower, the sole legal method to secure a government refund is by filing an ITR.
  • Carrying Forward Capital Losses: Short-term or long-term capital losses incurred on Indian mutual funds, shares, or real estate cannot be carried forward to offset future gains unless the return is filed on or before the statutory due date.
  • Seventh Proviso to Section 139(1) High-Value Transactions: Filing is mandatory if an NRI deposited over INR 1 crore in one or more current accounts, incurred electricity expenditure exceeding INR 1 lakh, or executed high-value financial transactions flagged in the Annual Information Statement (AIS).

3. Scope of Taxable Indian Income Under Section 5(2)

The cornerstone of non-resident taxation in India is codified in Section 5(2) of the Act. While a Resident and Ordinarily Resident (ROR) is taxed on worldwide income, the scope of tax for an NRI is strictly territorial:

Type of Income Source Taxable in India for NRI? Governing Statutory Provision
Overseas Salary & Employment Earnings Not Taxable Section 5(2) — Foreign-sourced earnings
Foreign Business Profits & Investments Not Taxable Section 5(2) — Accrued and received abroad
Rental Income from Indian Immovable Property Taxable Section 22–24 (Deemed to accrue in India)
Interest on NRO Savings / Fixed Deposits Taxable Section 56(2) — Subject to Section 195 TDS
Interest on NRE & FCNR Deposits 100% Tax-Free Section 10(4)(ii) statutory exemption
Capital Gains on Indian Real Estate / Securities Taxable Section 45 / Section 112 / Section 112A

It is critical to recognize that any income received directly in India or deemed to accrue or arise in India under Section 9 falls within the Indian tax net, regardless of where the contract was executed.

4. Old Tax Regime vs. New Tax Regime: Strategic Evaluation for NRIs

With the introduction and subsequent default status of the Concessional Tax Regime under Section 115BAC, NRIs must carefully evaluate which regime yields lower tax liability based on their Indian income structure:

The New Tax Regime (Section 115BAC – Default)

Offers lower, rationalized slab rates up to INR 15 lakhs and beyond, with a basic exemption limit of INR 3,00,000. However, it disallows most Chapter VI-A deductions (Section 80C, 80D) and standard deductions for housing loan interest on let-out properties under Section 24(b).

The Old Tax Regime (Optional via ITR Election)

Retains higher historical tax slabs but permits vital deductions commonly utilized by non-residents:

  • Section 24(a) Standard Deduction: Flat 30% statutory deduction on Net Annual Value of rental property.
  • Section 24(b) Home Loan Interest: Deductions for interest paid on Indian housing loans against rental yields.
  • Section 80C Deductions: Up to INR 1,50,000 for principal repayment of housing loans, life insurance premiums, tuition fees, and ELSS.
  • Section 80D Health Insurance: Deductions for health insurance premiums paid for resident senior citizen parents in India (up to INR 50,000).

Strategic Tip: For NRIs with high rental income and ongoing home loans in India, the Old Tax Regime frequently results in substantial tax savings compared to the default New Regime.

5. Selecting the Correct ITR Form (ITR-2 vs ITR-3)

A common pitfall among overseas taxpayers is selecting the simplified ITR-1 (Sahaj) or ITR-4 (Sugam). The Income Tax Department statutorily bars non-residents from filing ITR-1. Filing an invalid form results in a defective return notice under Section 139(9).

  • ITR-2 (Most Common for NRIs): Applicable for non-residents deriving income from Indian salary/pension, one or more house properties, capital gains (property, shares, mutual funds), and other sources (interest, dividends).
  • ITR-3: Mandatory for NRIs who earn income from a proprietorship, business, or professional practice operating in India.

6. Double Taxation Relief Under Section 90 & DTAA Protocol

Because non-residents reside in countries that tax global income (such as the United States or the United Kingdom), Indian-source income faces the threat of dual taxation. Under Section 90 of the Income-tax Act, India has executed comprehensive bilateral Double Taxation Avoidance Agreements (DTAAs) with over 85 nations.

Key treaty protections for NRIs include:

  • Capped Tax Rates on Interest & Royalty: Domestic Indian withholding tax on NRO interest is 30% plus cess. Under tax treaties with the USA, UK, Canada, Australia, and Singapore, the withholding rate is typically capped at 10% to 15%.
  • Foreign Tax Credit (FTC): Tax paid in India on property sales or rent can be offset against home-country tax liability using domestic foreign tax credit mechanisms (e.g., IRS Form 1116 in the USA, HMRC Foreign Tax Credit Relief in the UK).
Mandatory Compliance for Treaty Relief

To claim beneficial DTAA tax rates in an Indian return or lower withholding certificate, the NRI must furnish:

  1. A valid Tax Residency Certificate (TRC) issued by the tax revenue authority of their country of residence.
  2. An electronically submitted Form 10F generated on the official Indian income tax e-filing portal.
  3. A non-PE (Permanent Establishment) declaration confirming no business nexus in India.

7. High TDS Withholding & Claiming Refunds via Return Filing

Unlike resident taxpayers who benefit from modest TDS rates (1% to 10%), non-residents are subjected to aggressive statutory withholding under Section 195. Banks deduct 30% + cess on NRO account interest, and property buyers deduct 20% + surcharge + cess on the entire gross sale consideration of real estate.

Consequently, many NRIs find that the TDS deducted significantly exceeds their actual computed tax liability. For instance, if an NRI's total Indian income is INR 4,00,000, their actual tax liability may be negligible, yet the bank may have deducted INR 1,24,800.

Filing an Income Tax Return is the exclusive statutory mechanism to reclaim these excess deductions. Upon processing, the refund plus statutory interest under Section 244A (0.5% per month) is deposited directly into the taxpayer's pre-validated NRO or resident bank account.

8. Penalties, Section 148 Reassessment & Updated Returns (ITR-U)

With the integration of Project Insight, the Annual Information Statement (AIS), and automated banking data feeds, the Income Tax Department monitors high-value transactions involving non-residents with unprecedented precision. Failing to file an obligatory return exposes the taxpayer to severe legal consequences:

  • Late Filing Fees (Section 234F): Up to INR 5,000 for returns filed after the July 31 due date.
  • Penal Interest (Sections 234A, 234B, 234C): Charged at 1% per month on unpaid tax liability from the statutory deadline until payment.
  • Section 148 / 148A Reassessment Notices: If income escaping assessment exceeds INR 50 lakhs, the department can reopen assessments going back up to 5 to 10 years (subject to limitation reforms under the Income-tax Act, 2025).
  • Updated Return (ITR-U) Under Section 139(8A): For taxpayers who missed both the regular and belated deadlines, an ITR-U can be filed within 24 months from the end of the relevant assessment year, accompanied by an additional tax penalty of 25% to 50%.

9. Comprehensive Document Checklist for NRI E-Filing

Before initiating your Indian tax filing from abroad, ensure the following verified records are assembled:

NRI Return Preparation Checklist
  • Indian PAN Card: Active and updated Permanent Account Number.
  • Passport Copy & Movement History: Complete pages showing arrival and departure immigration stamps to calculate exact days in India under Section 6.
  • Bank Statements & Interest Certificates: Detailed annual statements for all NRE, NRO, and FCNR bank accounts held in India.
  • Form 26AS & AIS / TIS: Downloaded directly from the e-filing portal to reconcile all reported TDS, financial transactions, and asset sales.
  • Property Rental Agreements: Registered lease contracts, municipal property tax receipts, and housing loan interest certificates from Indian lenders.
  • Capital Gains Statements: Contract notes, broker statements, mutual fund capital gains ledgers, or sale deeds and Section 197 certificates for real estate.
  • Tax Residency Certificate (TRC) & Form 10F: Required if claiming treaty relief under an applicable DTAA.

10. Step-by-Step Filing Procedure on the Income Tax Portal

The complete filing workflow can be executed remotely from any overseas jurisdiction:

  1. Reconcile Portal Data: Log in to the official Income Tax portal (incometax.gov.in) and review Form 26AS, the Annual Information Statement (AIS), and Taxpayer Information Summary (TIS). Address any discrepancies before drafting.
  2. Draft the ITR-2 or ITR-3: Calculate gross total income across house property, capital gains, and interest schedules. Apply statutory deductions under Chapter VI-A or relevant treaty provisions.
  3. Reconcile TDS Credits: Verify that all Section 195 deductions match corresponding TAN entries on portal ledgers.
  4. Compute Final Liability / Refund: Generate the JSON computation file and inspect the tax summary.
  5. Electronic Verification (E-Verification): Non-residents can e-verify within 30 days via Net Banking (using an active NRO/NRE portal access), Indian bank account EVC, or by mailing a physically signed ITR-V acknowledgment form to the Centralized Processing Centre (CPC), Income Tax Department, Bengaluru, Karnataka 560500.
