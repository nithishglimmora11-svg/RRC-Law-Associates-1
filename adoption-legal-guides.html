Navigating Adoption Law in India with Legal Clarity

Adoption in India is a deeply transformative legal process governed by stringent statutory safeguards to protect the best interests of the child. Under Indian jurisprudence, there is no single uniform code; instead, the legal framework divides into secular statutory law and codified personal law. Attempting to adopt through informal agreements, unregistered deeds, or unauthorized intermediaries is strictly prohibited by law and carries severe criminal penalties under Section 80 of the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015.

The Three Pillars of Indian Adoption Jurisprudence

Understanding which legal regime applies to your family situation is the first and most critical legal step.

Juvenile Justice Act, 2015

The secular statutory framework applicable to all Indian citizens regardless of religion. Primarily governs the adoption of orphan, abandoned, surrendered, relative, and step-children. Grants statutory protection, mandates District Magistrate oversight, and accords full legal parentage.

Hindu Adoptions & Maintenance Act, 1956

Personal law governing Hindus, Buddhists, Jains, and Sikhs. Governs direct, consensual intra-family adoptions where biological parents physically give the child to adoptive parents. Does not apply to institutional orphan adoptions and requires strict adherence to Sections 6 to 11.

Adoption Regulations, 2022 & CARA

Notified by the Ministry of Women and Child Development under Section 68(c) of the JJ Act. Regulates in-country and inter-country workflows, home study timelines, portal seniority, SAA operations, foster agreements, and post-adoption reporting compliance.

Common legal inquiries addressed by our senior family and civil law practice.

A single male can only adopt a male child. In contrast, a single female applicant is legally eligible to adopt a child of any gender (boy or girl).
Under Section 61 of the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, as amended by the JJ Amendment Act, 2021, adoption applications under the Juvenile Justice framework (for orphan, abandoned, surrendered, relative, and step-children processed under the Act) are adjudicated by the District Magistrate (including Additional District Magistrates), with a statutory mandate for disposal within two months. In contrast, domestic consensual adoptions under the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 (HAMA) are completed through registered adoption deeds before the Sub-Registrar without requiring an order under Section 61. However, if prospective parents residing abroad adopt under HAMA and seek to relocate the child internationally, the District Magistrate conducts verification under Chapter VIII (Regulation 67) of Adoption Regulations, 2022.
No. Under both Section 15 of the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956, and the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015, a valid adoption is permanent and irrevocable. Neither the adoptive parents can renounce the child, nor can the biological parents reclaim the child once a valid adoption order has been passed or a valid registered adoption deed executed under law.
Direct private adoptions by foreign residents, NRIs, and OCIs without CARA oversight are generally prohibited under Section 56(4) of the JJ Act and the Hague Convention on Intercountry Adoption. If adopting a relative's child, the parties must register on the CARINGS portal under Chapter VI of Adoption Regulations 2022 and obtain a No Objection Certificate (NOC) from CARA before an adoption order can be granted and an immigration visa issued by the receiving country.
Under Section 80 of the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015, any person or organization that takes or gives a child in adoption without following the statutory provisions of the Act is liable to punitive imprisonment for up to 3 years, a fine of up to one lakh rupees, or both. Hospitals, nursing homes, and intermediaries involved in facilitating unlawful adoptions also face severe criminal liability.
