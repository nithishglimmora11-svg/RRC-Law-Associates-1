TDS Refund for NRIs in India: How to Claim Excess Tax Deducted

A step-by-step practitioner's guide to reconciling Form 26AS and AIS, applying for Section 197 Lower Deduction Certificates, claiming excess Section 195 deductions, and expediting CPC refund processing.

Authored by RRC Law Associates
Published: November 2026
15 min read
Verified for 2026 Compliance
Statutory claim workflows, Form 26AS reconciliation, and electronic refund processing under Indian income tax jurisprudence.

Non-Resident Indians may have tax deducted at source (TDS) from income earned in India. This may happen when they receive rental income, interest, investment income or proceeds from certain transactions.

Under Indian direct tax provisions, withholding tax rates on payments to non-residents are among the highest in the statute—ranging from 20% on long-term capital gains to over 30% on bank interest and rent. In many instances, the actual final tax liability of the NRI is substantially lower than the tax deducted at source, or even zero. Recovering these excess deductions requires an understanding of statutory claim procedures, portal reconciliations, and departmental protocols.

1. What Is TDS?

Tax Deducted at Source (TDS) is a withholding mechanism governed by Chapter XVII-B of the Income-tax Act, 1961. Under this framework, the payer of income (such as an Indian bank, a tenant, or a property purchaser) is legally mandated to deduct a specified percentage of tax before disbursing funds to the payee and deposit it directly with the Central Government.

For payments made to Non-Resident Indians, withholding is governed primarily by Section 195 of the Act. Unlike domestic TDS provisions (such as Section 194A for resident bank interest at 10%), Section 195 mandates deduction at "rates in force"—which typically means peak statutory rates (30% plus applicable surcharge and 4% Health & Education Cess), unless lowered by a specific treaty or statutory exemption certificate.

2. Why Might an NRI Have Excess TDS?

Non-residents frequently experience excessive tax withholding due to structural differences between gross withholding rules and net income tax liability:

  • Property Sales Withholding on Gross Sale Value: When an NRI sells immovable property in India, the buyer is required to deduct TDS under Section 195 on the entire gross sale consideration (at 20% + surcharge + cess for long-term capital assets), rather than on the actual net capital gain. If the property was inherited or purchased years ago, the actual capital gain—after accounting for indexed cost of acquisition or Section 54/54EC exemptions—may be a small fraction of the gross sale price, resulting in millions of rupees in excess tax withheld.
  • Total Income Below Basic Exemption Limit: An NRI whose total taxable Indian income is below the basic exemption limit (INR 2,50,000 under the old regime or INR 3,00,000 under the new regime) still suffers 30% TDS on NRO bank deposits and rental income. Since their net tax liability is zero, the entire TDS amount represents refundable excess tax.
  • Unclaimed Deductions and Treaty Benefits: Deductors cannot factor in Chapter VI-A deductions (Section 80C, 80D) or beneficial DTAA treaty rates unless prescribed forms and certificates are on record, leading to over-withholding.
  • Mutual Fund & Securities Redemptions: Peak withholding applied by asset management companies on mutual fund capital gains before final annual capital loss set-offs are computed.

3. How Can NRIs Claim an Income-Tax Refund?

Under Section 237 of the Income-tax Act, 1961, any taxpayer who has paid tax in excess of their actual legal liability is entitled to claim a refund of the surplus amount.

Exclusive Statutory Mechanism: Return Filing

There is no automatic refund procedure in Indian tax law. The exclusive legal method to claim a TDS refund is by filing an electronic Income Tax Return (ITR-2 or ITR-3) on the official e-filing portal (incometax.gov.in). Letter representations, emails to the Assessing Officer, or bank requests cannot trigger a refund disbursement.

When the return is processed by the Centralized Processing Centre (CPC) under Section 143(1), the system matches the TDS credits claimed in the return against government records (Form 26AS and AIS), determines the refundable balance, computes statutory interest under Section 244A, and issues an electronic credit order.

4. What Documents Are Required?

To prepare an accurate refund claim and satisfy CPC automated validation rules, an NRI must compile the following evidentiary documentation:

  1. Form 26AS (Tax Credit Statement): Downloaded from TRACES, confirming that deductors have deposited the withheld tax with the Government and tagged it to your PAN.
  2. Annual Information Statement (AIS) & Taxpayer Information Summary (TIS): Verifying financial transactions reported by banks, property registrars, and mutual fund houses.
  3. Form 16A / TDS Certificates: Issued quarterly by banks, tenants, and property purchasers detailing certificate numbers and Challan Identification Numbers (CIN).
  4. Property Transaction Documents (For Real Estate Sales): Registered Sale Deed, original purchase deed, cost improvement receipts, brokerage invoices, and Section 54/54EC investment receipts.
  5. Pre-Validated Indian Bank Account: Active NRO or resident bank account with matching PAN and name, successfully pre-validated on the e-filing portal for electronic refund transfer.

5. How Can NRIs Check TDS Credits?

Before submitting an ITR, non-residents must verify that all deducted tax is officially reflected in government databases:

Step 1: Access Form 26AS

Log in to incometax.gov.in, navigate to e-File → Income Tax Returns → View Form 26AS. Check Part A and Part A1 for deductions under Section 195 and Section 194-IA/IB.

Step 2: Reconcile AIS & TIS

Navigate to the AIS tab from the dashboard. Cross-check reported interest, mutual fund payouts, and high-value cash transactions against your actual bank statements.

What If TDS Is Missing From Form 26AS?

If tax was deducted from your payout but does not appear in Form 26AS, the deductor has likely either failed to deposit the tax or committed an error in filing their quarterly TDS return (such as entering an incorrect PAN). CPC algorithms will automatically disallow credit for missing entries. You must contact the deductor immediately to demand a revised TDS return (Form 27Q or 26QB) before filing your return.

6. Can an NRI Request Lower or Nil TDS?

Rather than waiting months to receive a refund after paying substantial excess tax, non-residents have a proactive statutory alternative under Section 197 of the Income-tax Act.

An NRI can apply online for a Lower or Nil Withholding Tax Certificate by filing Form 13 through the TRACES portal.

  • Pre-Transaction Timing: The Form 13 application must be submitted and approved before the property transaction is executed or payment is remitted. It cannot be applied retroactively to transactions already concluded.
  • Departmental Assessment: The Assessing Officer (International Taxation) examines projected capital gains calculations, acquisition costs, and estimated total income, issuing a formal certificate specifying a customized withholding rate (e.g., 3% or 5% instead of 20%).
  • Buyer Relief: Armed with this certificate, the buyer is legally authorized to deduct TDS only at the reduced certificate rate, saving the NRI from locking up millions of rupees in excess deductions.

7. How Can an NRI Receive a Refund?

Refund disbursement is entirely digital and automated through the Reserve Bank of India’s National Electronic Funds Transfer (NEFT) and Electronic Clearing System (ECS):

  1. Direct Credit to Pre-Validated Indian Bank Account: The standard, most reliable route is direct transfer into the NRI’s pre-validated NRO bank account in India.
  2. Foreign Bank Account Option: The Income Tax portal now allows non-residents who do not maintain an Indian bank account to enter international bank account details, including SWIFT code, International Bank Account Number (IBAN), and foreign bank address. However, processing international remittances often encounters intermediary banking delays.
  3. Statutory Interest on Refund: Section 244A: Under Section 244A, where a refund arises out of excess TDS and the return was filed on or before the due date under Section 139(1), the Government pays simple interest at the rate of 0.5% per month (6% per annum), calculated from 1 April of the assessment year to the date of refund grant.

8. What If the Refund Is Delayed or Not Received?

If an electronic refund status indicates failure, delay, or adjustment, follow this structured remediation protocol:

  • Check Refund Status on Portal: Review the tracker under e-File → Income Tax Returns → View Filed Returns → View Details. Common statuses include "Refund Failed", "Under Processing", or "Adjusted Against Outstanding Demand".
  • Resolve "Refund Failed" Errors: Usually caused by a name mismatch between PAN and Bank records, an unvalidated bank account, or an inactive account status. Re-validate the account on the portal and submit a formal "Refund Re-issue Request".
  • Respond to Section 245 Notice: If the department intends to adjust your refund against an alleged past tax demand, you have 30 days to agree or contest the demand online before the refund is offset.
  • File e-Nivaran Grievance: Escalate systemic delays through the portal’s electronic grievance redressal system.
  • Condonation of Delay Under Section 119(2)(b): If you failed to file your return within the belated return deadline due to genuine circumstances, you can file a formal condonation petition before the Chief Commissioner of Income Tax.

9. Important TDS and Refund Changes From 1 April 2026

Direct tax administration continues to undergo significant legislative reform:

Prospective Application of the Income-tax Act, 2025

The Income-tax Act, 2025 applies to tax years beginning on or after 1 April 2026. Earlier financial years (including FY 2024-25 and FY 2025-26) continue strictly under the Income-tax Act, 1961. Transitional preservation of refund claims and pending assessments is secured under Section 395(1).

Non-resident taxpayers should note that withholding rules under Section 195 and refund processes under Section 237 remain fully enforceable for all historical and ongoing tax years under the 1961 Act.
