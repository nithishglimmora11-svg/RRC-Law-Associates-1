Statutory Adoption Law in India

Adoption in India: Complete Legal Process, Eligibility and Requirements

An authoritative statutory guide on legal adoption in India, examining the distinction between the Juvenile Justice Act and HAMA, institutional CARA protocols, documentation standards, and the judicial role of the District Magistrate.

Last Updated: 21 November 2026 14 Min Read Statutory Analysis: JJ Act 2015 & Regulations 2022

Essential Legal Takeaways

Dual Legal Framework: Adoption in India operates through two distinct legal regimes: the secular, institutional framework of the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, and the personal law framework of the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 (HAMA).

Sole Statutory Authority: For institutional adoptions, the Central Adoption Resource Authority (CARA) is the nodal statutory body. All child allocations, home study reports, and child study dossiers must process via the government portal (CARINGS).

District Magistrate Jurisdiction (Section 61): Following the 2021 amendments to the Juvenile Justice Act, adoption proceedings under the JJ framework are adjudicated by the District Magistrate (DM) under Section 61 with a statutory mandate for disposal within two months. Domestic HAMA adoptions between resident Hindus are completed by registered deed and do not require an order under Section 61.

Criminalisation of Informal Transfers: Private, informal handovers of children, hospital baby-buying, or unregistered direct adoptions are severe criminal offenses under Section 80 of the JJ Act, punishable by up to 3 years imprisonment.

1. Introduction to Adoption in India

In Indian jurisprudence, adoption is defined under Section 2(2) of the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, as the legal process through which an adopted child is permanently separated from his or her biological parents and becomes the lawful child of the adoptive parents, with all the rights, privileges, and responsibilities that attach to a biological child.

The core objective of modern Indian adoption law has shifted decisively from property inheritance and spiritual continuity to child-centric welfare. The doctrine of the "best interests of the child"—enshrined in Section 3(iv) of the JJ Act—governs all statutory interpretations. Prospective Adoptive Parents (PAPs) seeking to build their family must navigate a highly regulated, transparent legal framework designed to prevent child trafficking, exploitation, and irregular adoptions.

2. Legal Framework Governing Adoption

Unlike Western legal systems where a single uniform civil code regulates all family matters, Indian adoption law is characterized by two distinct legal routes. Understanding which regime applies to your personal situation is fundamental before initiating any legal steps.

The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015

The JJ Act 2015 is a secular, progressive statute that applies uniformly to any Indian citizen, regardless of their religious faith—including Hindus, Muslims, Christians, Parsis, and Jains. Under Section 56(1) of the Act, adoption is mandated to secure the right to a family for orphan, abandoned, and surrendered children (OAS), as well as relative and step-parent adoptions.

Procedural enforcement under the JJ Act is carried out by the Adoption Regulations, 2022, notified by the Ministry of Women and Child Development (MWCD). These regulations prescribe the timelines, Home Study Report (HSR) parameters, eligibility scores, and online portal processing rules.

The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 (HAMA)

HAMA is personal codified legislation applicable exclusively to Hindus, Buddhists, Jains, and Sikhs. Section 56(3) of the JJ Act 2015 explicitly provides a statutory savings clause: "Nothing in this Act shall apply to the adoption of children made under the provisions of the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956."

HAMA governs direct, private adoptions within the Hindu community where biological parents physically give the child in adoption to adoptive parents. It does not provide for the adoption of institutional orphan or abandoned children. An adoption under HAMA is executed through a formal, registered Adoption Deed under Section 16, subject to strict statutory requisites under Sections 6 to 11.

3. Difference Between JJ Act and HAMA

Selecting the correct statutory route is essential. An attempted adoption executed under the wrong legal statute is void ab initio (void from the beginning) and leaves the adoptive parents vulnerable to criminal prosecution or custody disputes.

4. Types of Adoption in India

Depending on family circumstances, adoption cases in India fall into four principal statutory categories:

  • In-Country Institutional Adoption: Resident Indian citizens adopting an unrelated orphan, abandoned, or surrendered child who has been declared legally free for adoption by the Child Welfare Committee (CWC).
  • Relative Adoption: Prospective parents adopting a child of their relatives (defined strictly under Section 2(52) of the JJ Act as paternal uncle/aunt, maternal uncle/aunt, or grandparents).
  • Step-Parent Adoption: A step-parent adopting the biological child of their spouse from a prior marriage or relationship, requiring biological parent consent.
  • Inter-Country Adoption: Non-Resident Indians (NRIs), Overseas Citizens of India (OCIs), or foreign nationals habitually residing abroad adopting a child from India under the safeguards of the 1993 Hague Convention.

5. Who Can Adopt a Child?

Statutory eligibility under Section 57 of the JJ Act and Regulation 5 of the Adoption Regulations, 2022, requires prospective adoptive parents to satisfy composite age, health, and financial benchmarks:

  • Physical, Mental & Financial Stability: The applicants must be physically fit, mentally alert, emotionally sound, and have adequate financial resources to provide for the child.
  • Marital Status & Stability: Married couples must have at least 2 years of stable marital relationship. Both spouses must give joint written consent.
  • Single Parent Rules: A single female is legally entitled to adopt a child of any gender. However, under Section 57(4) of the JJ Act, a single male is strictly barred from adopting a female child.
  • Age Difference: There must be a minimum age difference of 25 years between the prospective adoptive parent and the child (subject to statutory relaxations in relative and step-parent adoptions).

6. Role of CARA and State Adoption Agencies

The statutory architecture under Chapter VIII of the JJ Act relies on coordinated institutional bodies to eliminate illicit commercial transactions:

Central Adoption Resource Authority (CARA)

Established as an autonomous statutory body under Section 68 of the JJ Act. Regulates in-country adoptions and functions as the Central Authority for inter-country adoptions under the 1993 Hague Convention.

Specialised Adoption Agency (SAA)

State-licensed child care institutions authorized under Section 65 to rehabilitate orphan, abandoned, or surrendered children and prepare Home Study Reports (HSR) for prospective parents.

District Child Protection Unit (DCPU)

The district-level administrative body that conducts family verifications, assists in relative adoptions, and maintains adoption records under the District Magistrate.

Child Welfare Committee (CWC)

A quasi-judicial statutory committee under Section 27 that conducts inquiry and formally declares an orphan, abandoned, or surrendered child legally free for adoption under Section 38.

7. Step-by-Step Adoption Process

The institutional adoption workflow under the Adoption Regulations, 2022, progresses through eight structured statutory phases:

1

Online Registration on CARINGS

Prospective parents register online on the official CARA portal (carings.nic.in), uploading initial KYC documents, identity proofs, and photographs to create their unique seniority profile.

2

Selection of SAA & Home Study Report (HSR)

PAPs choose a licensed Specialised Adoption Agency (SAA) in their home district. A certified social worker visits their residence, assesses socio-economic conditions, and submits the Home Study Report within sixty days from the date of submission of requisite documents on the Designated Portal, as prescribed under Regulation 10(8). Under Regulation 10(10), the HSR remains valid for three years.

3

Seniority Generation & Referral

Once the HSR is approved on CARINGS, seniority is fixed. When a child matching the age preference is declared legally free, CARINGS generates an algorithmic referral of up to two children.

4

In-Country 48-Hour Reservation (Regulation 11(3))

Under Regulation 11(3), in-country prospective adoptive parents have forty-eight hours to inspect the Child Study Report (CSR) and Medical Examination Report (MER) and reserve the child on the Designated Portal. Matching and acceptance are conducted separately under Regulation 12, followed by foster care under Regulation 13. (Referral timelines for overseas PAPs are governed separately under inter-country adoption provisions).

5

Physical Meeting & Foster Care Agreement

PAPs visit the specialized adoption agency to meet the child. Upon mutual bonding and formal acceptance, a Pre-Adoption Foster Care Agreement is signed under Regulation 13, allowing the child to live with the PAPs pending legal orders.

6

Filing Adoption Petition before District Magistrate

The SAA files an adoption application under Section 61 of the JJ Act before the District Magistrate (DM) within the territorial jurisdiction where the agency is situated.

7

In-Camera Hearing & Certified Adoption Order

The District Magistrate conducts in-camera proceedings to verify statutory consents and child welfare safeguards. The final Adoption Order is passed within 60 days of filing.

8

New Birth Certificate & Post-Adoption Follow-Up

The local birth registrar issues a revised birth certificate naming the adoptive parents as legal parents. The SAA submits bi-annual progress reports for 2 years.

To understand the comprehensive digital mechanics of CARINGS, explore our guide on the CARA Adoption Process in India: Registration, Home Study and Adoption Order.

8. Documents Required

A flawless adoption dossier prevents administrative rejections. All documents uploaded to CARINGS must be notarized, attested, and clearly legible.

Current family photograph of the prospective adoptive parents
PAN Card, Aadhaar Card, or valid Passports for identity verification
Birth certificates or school leaving certificates establishing age
Registered Marriage Certificate (if married) or divorce decree/death certificate
Medical fitness certificate issued by a registered medical practitioner (MBBS)
Income proof: Form 16, Salary Slips, ITR for the last 3 assessment years
Two recommendation letters from respectable acquaintances
Consent of older children (above 5 years of age) in the family

9. Adoption Order and Legal Consequences

Under Section 61 of the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, as amended by the Juvenile Justice Amendment Act, 2021, the jurisdiction to adjudicate adoption applications under the Juvenile Justice framework was transferred from Civil District Courts to the District Magistrate (DM) and Additional District Magistrates (ADM). This statutory amendment applies to adoption proceedings under the JJ Act (concerning orphan, abandoned, surrendered, relative, and step-children processed under the Act), mandating disposal within two months. It does not govern domestic Hindu adoptions under HAMA, which are completed via registered deeds without requiring a Section 61 order.

Under the amended Section 61 of the JJ Act, all adoption jurisdiction was transferred to the District Magistrate (DM) and Additional District Magistrates (ADM). The statute explicitly mandates:

  • Two-Month Disposal Mandate: Under Section 61(2) of the JJ Act, adoption proceedings shall be held in camera and the application shall be disposed of by the District Magistrate within a period of two months from the date of filing.
  • In-Camera Proceedings: To preserve family privacy and protect the identity of the minor, hearings are held in-camera without public presence.
  • Irrevocability: The resulting Adoption Order is final and irrevocable. Under Section 63 of the JJ Act, the child legally severs ties with the biological family and inherits all succession rights in the adoptive family.

Statutory Excerpt: Section 63 of the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015

"The adoption shall be final and binding on the adoptive parents and the child, with effect from the date of the adoption order, and from that date the child shall be deemed to be the child of the adoptive parents or of the adoptive parent, with all the rights, privileges and obligations attached to a biological child."

10. Common Legal Mistakes & Criminal Liabilities

Navigating adoption without qualified legal guidance often leads families into dangerous procedural traps:

  • Purchasing Children from Hospitals or Midwives: Believing that private payments can expedite family formation. This constitutes human trafficking under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (formerly IPC) and the JJ Act.
  • Executing an Unregistered Deed for Abandoned Children: Trying to execute a HAMA deed for an orphan or institutional child. HAMA deeds are legally void when the child is not physically given by natural Hindu parents.
  • Ignoring Section 80 Penalties: Taking custody without CARA approval or without DM orders exposes both giver and receiver to up to 3 years rigorous imprisonment.

Criminal Liability Warning: Section 80 JJ Act, 2015

"If any person, organisation or child care institution takes or offers to take, or gives or offers to give, any child in adoption without following the provisions or procedures as provided in this Act, such person or organisation shall be punishable with imprisonment which may extend to three years or with fine of one lakh rupees or with both."

11. How Legal Assistance Can Help

While CARA manages matching and placement, qualified legal counsel is indispensable for safeguarding adoptive families against technical defects:

  • Legal Route Determination: Conducting preliminary legal due diligence to confirm whether your adoption requires CARINGS registration, HAMA deed drafting, or inter-country Hague compliance.
  • Dossier Pre-Audit: Scrutinizing medical fitness affidavits, overseas attestation, apostilles, and financial records to prevent CARINGS rejections.
  • Drafting DM Applications: Structuring formal adoption petitions before the District Magistrate and drafting biological parental consent affidavits in relative adoptions.
  • Civil & Succession Estate Planning: Advising on will drafting, family trusts, and property succession to ensure the adopted child enjoys undisputed coparcenary and ancestral inheritance rights.

13. Conclusion

Adoption in India provides a noble and legally permanent pathway to parenthood. However, because it alters fundamental legal status and civil rights, the procedure requires absolute adherence to statutory mandates. Whether processing through CARINGS under the Juvenile Justice Act or executing a family adoption under HAMA, securing experienced legal counsel ensures your child's legal security and protects your family from irreversible procedural defects.
