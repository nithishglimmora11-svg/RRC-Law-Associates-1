Customs Investigation: Rights During Search, Summons and Examination

Navigating Section 105 Searches, Section 108 Summons, Evidentiary Value of Statements, and Constitutional Protections

13 min read
Reviewed by RRC Customs Litigation Practice
Updated November 2026

Key Takeaways & At a Glance

  • A summons issued under Section 108 of the Customs Act, 1962, is deemed a judicial proceeding under Sections 193 and 228 of the Indian Penal Code (BNS); giving false evidence or refusing to attend attracts criminal prosecution.
  • Statements recorded under Section 108 are legally admissible evidence in court, unlike confessions given to ordinary police officers under the Indian Evidence Act.
  • You have the constitutional right to be treated with dignity and fairness under Article 21. Summons cannot be used as an instrument of mental or physical coercion, and questioning cannot continue overnight indefinitely.
  • Under the landmark Supreme Court ruling in Poolpandi v. B.I.R. (1992), while you do not have an absolute right to have an advocate whisper answers, your legal counsel is permitted to be present within visible (though not audible) distance during interrogation.
  • If a statement is extorted under duress, threat, or physical coercion, a formal retraction must be filed immediately via a sworn affidavit before a Judicial Magistrate and served on the investigating authority at the earliest opportunity.

Understanding the Legal Issue

Facing a customs investigation initiated by the Directorate of Revenue Intelligence (DRI), the Special Intelligence and Investigation Branch (SIIB), or the Customs Preventive Wing is an intensely stressful experience for corporate executives, importers, and customs brokers. Investigations typically begin with an unannounced search of commercial premises under Section 105 or the issuance of a formal summons under Section 108 requiring personal attendance to give evidence and produce financial ledgers.

Unlike conventional civil litigation, customs investigations carry criminal overtones. Because statements recorded by gazetted customs officers under Section 108 are admissible in evidence before criminal courts, individuals summoned often compromise their legal defense unknowingly by signing inaccurate statements prepared by investigating officers. Understanding your statutory rights, the legal boundaries of investigative powers, the exact evidentiary value of Section 108 statements, and the strict procedure for retracting coerced admissions is vital to protecting your commercial reputation and personal liberty.

Key Provisions of the Customs Act, 1962

Customs proceedings in India are governed strictly by codified parliamentary statutes and binding notifications issued by the Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs (CBIC). The foundational statutory provisions applicable to this matter include:

Section 105 Customs Act, 1962

Empowers the Assistant/Deputy Commissioner to authorize the search of any place if there is 'reason to believe' that dutiable or prohibited goods or relevant documents are secreted therein.

Section 108 Customs Act, 1962

Authorizes gazetted officers of customs to summon any person whose attendance is considered necessary to give evidence or produce documents; deemed judicial proceedings.

Article 20(3) Constitution of India

Fundamental right guaranteeing that no person accused of any offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself.

Article 21 Constitution of India

Right to life and personal liberty, guaranteeing protection against custodial violence, night-long coercive interrogation, and arbitrary detention.

In-Depth Legal Analysis & Practical Application

1. Search of Premises under Section 105: Panchnama Protocol

Under Section 105, a search warrant (search authorization) can only be executed if supported by recorded reasons. Key legal rights during a premises search include:

  • Production of Search Authorization: Officers must produce the written search authorization signed by an officer not below the rank of Assistant Commissioner.
  • Independent Panchas (Witnesses): Two independent, respectable local inhabitants must witness the entire search from start to finish.
  • Personal Search of Officers: The occupants of the premises have the right to request that customs officers submit to a physical search before entering to ensure no evidence is planted.
  • Copy of Panchnama: A complete, legible copy of the Panchnama listing every seized electronic device, hard drive, ledger, and file must be handed over to the occupant upon conclusion.

2. The Mechanics of Section 108 Summons

A summons under Section 108 must clearly specify the inquiry and cannot be issued as a routine administrative inquiry. The summoned person must state the truth upon any subject respecting which they are examined. Crucially:

  • You are entitled to state that you do not recall specific transactions off-hand and require access to accounting records to verify facts.
  • You are not legally obliged to make assumptions, guess trade values, or confirm theoretical calculations prepared by investigating officers.
  • Interrogations cannot continue throughout the night; officers must conduct questioning during reasonable business hours.

3. Legal Procedure for Retraction of Statements

If an investigating team coerces a noticee into signing pre-drafted statements admitting undervaluation or duty evasion under threat of immediate arrest:

1. The noticee must file a sworn retraction affidavit at the very first opportunity—ideally within 24 to 48 hours.

2. The affidavit must detail the exact circumstances of coercion, hours of questioning, denial of medical attention, and specific repudiation of the involuntary statements.

3. The retraction should be dispatched via registered speed post to the Commissioner of Customs, the head of the investigating agency (DRI/DGGI), and submitted directly before the jurisdictional Judicial Magistrate if arrest proceedings are initiated.

Essential Documents & Evidence Checklist

To substantiate claims, establish commercial bonafides, and refute departmental allegations, assemble the following documentary evidence:

  • Original Summons notice under Section 108 and proof of service
  • Copy of Search Authorization and signed Panchnama (if premises searched)
  • Inventory of all seized electronic devices, laptops, mobile phones, and ledgers
  • Corporate authorization letter / Board Resolution authorizing appearance
  • Medical certificates in case of pre-existing health conditions requiring reasonable accommodation
  • Sworn Retraction Affidavit (if involuntary statement was recorded)

Practical Case Scenario

Scenario: The Managing Director of a Tuticorin-based spice export-import firm was summoned by the DRI to their regional headquarters regarding alleged undervaluation of imported black pepper. Questioning began at 11:00 AM and continued past midnight. Exhausted and denied his prescribed hypertension medication, the Director was made to sign a pre-typed statement admitting that ₹85 Lakhs had been settled through non-banking channels.

Legal Resolution: The following morning, RRC Law Associates had the Director examined at a government hospital to document his elevated blood pressure and sleep deprivation. Our legal team drafted and dispatched a comprehensive sworn retraction affidavit to the Principal Additional Director General (DRI) and the Judicial Magistrate within 24 hours of release. We established that the statement was procured through coercive exhaustion in violation of Article 21. During subsequent adjudication, the Adjudicating Commissioner ruled that the retracted statement lacked corroborative documentary evidence, thereby dropping the proposed prosecution.

Common Legal Pitfalls to Avoid

Critical Procedural Mistakes

  • Failing to attend after receiving a Section 108 summons without filing an official request for adjournment on medical or emergency grounds.
  • Signing blank sheets or signing statements without reading every single word and correcting inaccuracies.
  • Admitting to hypothetical legal conclusions (e.g., agreeing that goods are 'misdeclared') rather than stating objective facts.
  • Delaying the filing of a retraction for weeks or months, which severely undermines its legal credibility before courts.

Conclusion & Strategic Advice

Customs law disputes in India require swift, technically informed, and legally verified advocacy. Because customs authorities operate under stringent statutory limitation deadlines—from the 30-day Show Cause Notice window to the strict 60-day appellate period under Section 128—delay or informal verbal negotiations frequently compromise substantive legal rights.

Engaging specialized customs legal counsel at the earliest stage of search, summons, seizure, or notice issuance ensures that your business preserves documentary evidence, maintains procedural compliance, and enforces all statutory protections guaranteed under Indian law.
