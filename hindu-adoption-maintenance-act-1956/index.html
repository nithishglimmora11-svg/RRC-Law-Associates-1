Adoption Under Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956: Legal Rules and Conditions

An authoritative statutory treatise on Chapter II of HAMA 1956, examining mandatory requisites under Section 6, male and female capacity, biological parental consent, statutory prohibitions, registered adoption deeds, and judicial presumptions.

Last Updated: 21 November 2026 15 Min Read Statutory Framework: Act No. 78 of 1956

Essential HAMA Takeaways

Exclusivity of the Act (Section 5): Any adoption made by a Hindu after 1956 must strictly comply with the Act. Any adoption executed in contravention of HAMA is void ab initio and confers no legal status or inheritance rights.

Living Child Prohibition (Section 11): Adoptive parents cannot adopt a son if they already have a living Hindu son, son's son, or son's son's son. Similarly, they cannot adopt a daughter if they have a living Hindu daughter or son's daughter.

The 21-Year Age Difference Mandate: If a male adopts a female, or a female adopts a male, the adoptive parent must be at least 21 years older than the child. Failure to satisfy this age gap renders the adoption void.

Section 16 Presumption: A registered adoption deed signed by both the biological giver and the adoptive taker raises a statutory presumption of validity under Section 16 until disproved by rebuttal evidence.

1. Introduction to HAMA and Historical Context

Prior to the codification of Hindu law in 1956, adoption was governed by uncodified Shastric Hindu jurisprudence, which viewed adoption primarily as a sacramental institution (dattaka). The classical Shastric objective was twofold: to secure the performance of funeral and spiritual rites (pinda-dan) for the adoptive father's spiritual salvation, and to perpetuate the male patrilineal lineage. Under Shastric law, only males could be adopted, females could not adopt in their own right, and daughters could never be taken in adoption.

The enactment of the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 (Act No. 78 of 1956) fundamentally modernized Hindu family law. It secularized adoption, permitted the adoption of girls, conferred legal capacity on women, and abolished the caste barrier. Today, adoption under HAMA is a statutory contract governed by Chapter II (Sections 5 to 17), operating independently alongside the secular Juvenile Justice Act, 2015 framework as preserved by Section 56(3) of the JJ Act.

2. Applicability of HAMA (Section 2)

Under Section 2 of HAMA, the statute applies to any person who is:

  • A Hindu by religion in any of its forms or developments, including Virashaivas, Lingayats, or followers of the Brahmo, Prarthana, or Arya Samaj.
  • A Buddhist, Jain, or Sikh by religion.
  • Any other person residing in India who is not a Muslim, Christian, Parsi, or Jew by religion, unless it is proved that they would not have been governed by Hindu law.

Both the person adopting and the child being adopted must be Hindu at the time of the adoption. If a Hindu adopts a non-Hindu child, or if a non-Hindu attempts an adoption under HAMA, the adoption is an absolute nullity.

3. Section 5: Mandatory Character of the Act

Section 5 of HAMA establishes the absolute supremacy of statutory law over custom:

  • Section 5(1): No adoption shall be made after the commencement of this Act by or to a Hindu except in accordance with the provisions contained in this Chapter. Any adoption made in contravention thereof shall be void.
  • Section 5(2): An adoption which is void shall neither create any rights in the adoptive family in favor of any person which he could not have acquired except by reason of such adoption, nor destroy the rights of any person in the family of his birth.

Therefore, custom, local usage, or informal family understandings cannot override the express statutory mandates of Sections 6 to 11 of the Act.

4. Section 6: Requisites of a Valid Adoption

Section 6 is the foundational provision of HAMA. It prescribes four cumulative conditions, all of which must be concurrently satisfied for an adoption to be valid in law:

Clause (i): Capacity of the Adopter

The person adopting must have the legal capacity and also the right to take in adoption as prescribed under Section 7 (male) or Section 8 (female).

Clause (ii): Capacity of the Giver

The person giving the child in adoption must have the legal capacity to do so under Section 9 (natural biological father, mother, or guardian).

Clause (iii): Capacity of the Child

The person being adopted must be capable of being taken in adoption under Section 10 (unmarried, under 15 years, Hindu, not already adopted).

Clause (iv): Compliance with Other Conditions

The adoption must be completed in strict compliance with the other mandatory statutory conditions enumerated under Section 11.

5. Section 7: Capacity of a Male Hindu to Adopt

Under Section 7 of HAMA, any male Hindu who is of sound mind and is not a minor (i.e., has completed 18 years of age) has the legal capacity to take a son or a daughter in adoption:

  • Mandatory Consent of Wife: If the male Hindu is married, he cannot adopt without the consent of his wife. If he has more than one living wife, the consent of all wives is necessary.
  • Exceptions Dispensing with Consent: The wife's consent is dispensed with only if she has:
    • Completely and finally renounced the world (taken religious sanyas);
    • Ceased to be a Hindu (converted to another religion); or
    • Been declared by a court of competent jurisdiction to be of unsound mind.

An adoption executed by a married male Hindu without his wife's consent is void ab initio, as affirmed by the Supreme Court in Kashibai v. Parwatibai (1995).

6. Section 8: Capacity of a Female Hindu to Adopt

Historically, Section 8 permitted a female Hindu to adopt only if she was unmarried, widowed, or divorced. A married woman could not adopt in her own right during the subsistence of her marriage.

This gender disparity was eliminated by the Personal Laws (Amendment) Act, 2010 (Act No. 30 of 2010). Following the amendment, Section 8 provides:

  • Any female Hindu who is of sound mind and not a minor has the capacity to take a son or daughter in adoption.
  • If she is married, she may adopt with the consent of her husband.
  • The husband's consent is dispensed with only if he has renounced the world, ceased to be a Hindu, or been adjudged of unsound mind by a court.

Consequently, male and female Hindus today enjoy equal statutory capacity to adopt.

7. Section 9: Persons Capable of Giving in Adoption

Under Section 9 of HAMA, no person other than the biological father, biological mother, or legal guardian has the capacity to give a child in adoption:

  • Equal Rights of Father and Mother: Post-2010 amendment, the father and mother have equal rights to give a child in adoption. Neither can give the child without the consent of the other, unless one spouse has renounced the world, converted, or been declared of unsound mind.
  • Role of Guardian: Where both parents are dead, have renounced the world, or have abandoned the child, the legal guardian of the child may give the child in adoption with the prior permission of the court under Section 9(4) of HAMA. (A domestic HAMA adoption between resident Hindus is completed by registered deed and does not require an adoption order under Section 61 of the JJ Act).

8. Section 10: Persons Who May Be Adopted

To be legally capable of being adopted under Section 10, the child must satisfy four negative conditions:

Statutory Condition Section 10 Mandate Customary Exception
Religion Must be a Hindu (Buddhist, Jain, Sikh) No exception; non-Hindus cannot be adopted under HAMA
Prior Adoption Must not have already been adopted No exception; double adoption is strictly void
Marital Status Must not be married Permitted only if a recognized custom or usage applicable to the parties allows married adoptions
Age Limit Must not have completed 15 years of age Permitted only if custom or usage allows adoption of persons aged 15 or above

9. Section 11: Other Mandatory Conditions for Valid Adoption

Section 11 sets out strict procedural and family composition safeguards that must not be violated:

  • No Living Son (Section 11(i)): If adopting a son, the adoptive father or mother must not have a Hindu son, son's son, or son's son's son (whether by legitimate blood or by prior adoption) living at the time of adoption.
  • No Living Daughter (Section 11(ii)): If adopting a daughter, the adoptive parent must not have a Hindu daughter or daughter's daughter (son's daughter) living at the time of adoption.
  • 21-Year Age Difference (Section 11(iii) & (iv)):
    • If a male adopts a female child: The adoptive father must be at least 21 years older than the girl.
    • If a female adopts a male child: The adoptive mother must be at least 21 years older than the boy.
  • No Simultaneous Adoption (Section 11(v)): The same child cannot be adopted simultaneously by two or more persons (other than a husband and wife jointly).
  • Actual Physical Giving and Taking (Section 11(vi)): The child must be actually given and taken in adoption by the biological and adoptive parents with the intent to transfer the child from the family of birth to the family of adoption. Performance of religious ceremonies like Datta Homam is not essential, but physical delivery is mandatory.

Landmark Ruling: M. Gurudas v. Rasaranjan (Supreme Court, 2006)

"To constitute a valid adoption, the physical act of giving and taking is an imperative condition. A mere registered deed of adoption without actual delivery of the child does not confer the legal status of an adopted son or daughter."

10. Section 12: Legal Effects of a Valid Adoption

Section 12 details the legal transformation of the child upon completion of a valid adoption:

  • Severance from Family of Birth: An adopted child is deemed to be the child of his or her adoptive father or mother for all purposes with effect from the date of adoption. All ties with the biological family are severed.
  • Extinguishment of Maintenance: Natural parents have no further right to claim maintenance or custody of the child.
  • Full Coparcenary & Inheritance Rights: The child acquires coparcenary status in ancestral property and equal inheritance rights in the adoptive family alongside any biological children subsequently born.
  • Proviso (a): Prohibited Degrees of Relationship: The adopted child cannot marry any person whom he or she could not have married if they had continued in the family of birth (preventing incestuous unions).
  • Proviso (b): Vested Property: Any property that had already vested in the adopted child prior to adoption (e.g., through inheritance from a deceased relative) remains with the child.
  • Proviso (c): Divesting Prohibited: The adopted child shall not divest any person of any estate which vested in him or her before the adoption.

11. Irrevocability, Presumption, and Consideration

Section 15: Valid Adoption Cannot Be Cancelled

Under Section 15, no adoption which has been validly made can be cancelled by the adoptive father or mother or any other person, nor can the adopted child renounce his or her status as such and return to the family of birth.

Section 16: Legal Presumption as to Registered Documents

Under Section 16, whenever any document registered under any law relating to the registration of documents is produced before any court purporting to record an adoption made and is signed by the person giving and the person taking the child in adoption, the court shall presume that the adoption was made in compliance with the provisions of this Act unless and until it is disproved.

Section 17: Prohibition of Certain Payments

Section 17 strictly prohibits any person from receiving or giving any payment or other reward in consideration of adoption. Any contravention is punishable with imprisonment for up to six months, or fine, or both.

12. Drafting and Registering a Valid HAMA Adoption Deed

To ensure that an adoption deed enjoys the statutory presumption under Section 16, it must be drafted with precision:

Accurate recitals of Hindu religion of both natural and adoptive parents
Affirmation that adopters have no living Hindu child of the same gender
Verification of 21-year age difference where child is of opposite sex
Written spousal consent recital for both adoptive and natural parents
Recital of the actual physical act of giving and taking in the presence of witnesses
Negative declaration confirming no financial consideration or reward paid
Payment of appropriate state stamp duty as prescribed under State Stamp Act
Registration before Sub-Registrar within the territorial jurisdiction

14. Conclusion

The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956, provides a dignified, traditional pathway for intra-family adoption among Hindus, Buddhists, Jains, and Sikhs. However, because statutory requirements under Sections 6 through 11 are rigid and non-negotiable, any technical omission in deed drafting or consent verification can dismantle the adoption decades later during inheritance partition suits. Engaging a seasoned family law firm to audit compliance, draft the deed, and oversee registration ensures that your family's legal bond remains unassailable.
